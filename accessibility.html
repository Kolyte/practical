Why it matters

Online is where life happens now.

As more of everyday life moves online, from banking to booking a GP appointment, a website that only works with a mouse, a screen and sound shuts people out of services everyone else takes for granted.

Computing is part of the problem, but it is also a large part of the answer. The tools below turn text into speech, speech into text, and give people new ways to control a computer when a mouse and keyboard do not work for them.

Screen Speech

Screen readers

A screen reader reads out what is on the screen, or sends it to a refreshable braille display.

Rather than reading pixels, it uses the structure of the page: headings, links, buttons, form labels and image descriptions. Users move around with keyboard shortcuts or touch gestures, jumping from heading to heading or listing every link on a page. Tabbing to the menu at the top of this page sounds something like this:

Screen readers used to be expensive specialist software. Today they are built into every major platform: VoiceOver on Apple devices, TalkBack on Android and Narrator on Windows. NVDA, an open-source screen reader for Windows started in 2006 by two blind developers in Australia, is free to download and is now one of the most widely used in the world.

In 2009 the iPhone 3GS became the first touchscreen phone with a built-in screen reader.

Before then many assumed a flat glass screen could never work for blind users. VoiceOver showed that it could.

Speech Text

Live captioning

Automatic speech recognition turns spoken words into text in real time, like the caption at the top of this page.

It uses machine learning models trained on huge amounts of recorded speech. For deaf and hard of hearing people this means captions on video calls, lectures, phone calls and conversations in the street, not just on pre-captioned TV.

Android's Live Caption and Apple's Live Captions caption any audio playing on the device, and much of the processing now happens on the phone itself, so nothing has to be sent to a server. Teams, Zoom and Google Meet all offer automatic captions, and many universities use them on recorded lectures.

Captions help far more people than those who cannot hear.

Anyone watching in a noisy room, anyone listening in a second language, and anyone who simply remembers more when they read along.

Voice Device

Speech technology

Speech works in both directions: people talk to their devices, and devices talk for people.

Voice control lets people with limited movement operate a computer or phone entirely by speaking: opening apps, dictating emails and clicking buttons by name. Text-to-speech gives a voice to people who cannot speak, through augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices, the best known being the synthesiser used by Stephen Hawking.

Voice banking lets someone diagnosed with a condition such as motor neurone disease record their own voice while they still can, so their AAC device later speaks in a voice that sounds like them rather than a generic one. Apps such as Be My Eyes connect blind users with sighted volunteers over video, and now offer an AI assistant that describes photos aloud.

Eye-tracking and switch access complete the picture, giving people who cannot speak or use their hands a way to type and control a device with their gaze or a single button.

For developers

Building accessible software

Assistive technology only works if the software it talks to is built properly.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), published by the W3C, set out how. They are organised around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. In practice, a lot of it comes down to a few habits:

  • Use real HTML elements (<button>, <nav>, headings in order) so screen readers understand the page.
  • Give every meaningful image a text alternative with the alt attribute.
  • Make sure everything works with a keyboard alone, with a visible focus outline.
  • Keep enough contrast between text and background, and never rely on colour alone.
  • Caption videos and provide transcripts for audio.

In the UK, public sector websites must meet WCAG under the Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018. This page tries to follow it too: reload and press Tab to find the skip link.

Still to fix

Challenges

Most websites still fall short, and every gap is a barrier.

Missing image descriptions, low-contrast text and unlabelled form fields remain common. Speech recognition is also less accurate for people with strong regional accents, non-native speakers and people whose speech is affected by a disability, often the people who need it most.

Cost is another barrier. Mainstream phones have made a lot of assistive technology free, but braille displays and specialist AAC devices can still cost thousands of pounds.

The best results come when disabled people help design the technology from the start.
