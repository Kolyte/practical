Computing for a cooler planet

Climate change is a physical problem, but much of our understanding of it, and many of the tools for tackling it, come from computers.

See how

Three ways computing helps

From predicting the future to managing the electricity we use today.

Climate modelling

Climate models split the atmosphere and oceans into a grid and simulate how heat, air and water move between the cells. Running them takes some of the largest supercomputers in the world, and the results inform forecasts and government policy.

Weather services such as the Met Office publish a lot of this work openly.

Smart energy grids

Wind and solar output changes with the weather, so grids need software to balance supply and demand minute by minute. Forecasting, smart meters and battery scheduling all help renewable power replace fossil fuels without blackouts.

The Carbon Intensity API shows how green Great Britain's electricity is right now.

Monitoring from space

Satellites photograph the whole planet every few days. Image analysis can spot illegal logging, track ice loss and measure crop health far faster than people on the ground could.

Global Forest Watch turns this data into a free interactive map.

Why it matters

Decisions about energy, farming and flood defences are expensive and slow to reverse. Simulations and live data let planners test options before committing, and spot problems earlier.

There is a catch, though: large models and data centres use electricity themselves. Making computing more efficient, and running it on clean power, is part of the same challenge.
